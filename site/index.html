garmin-freelap

An open-source Garmin Connect IQ watch app that reads a Freelap FxChip BLE and writes every transmitter crossing into your activity file.

Status: in development. This has never been run against a real Freelap chip.

Freelap have since provided the broadcast frame specification, and the app decodes it — checked against the worked example in that document. But nobody has yet timed twenty reps on a track against MyFreelap, and one question is still open that decides what this can be: whether a Garmin watch can read the part of the broadcast carrying the splits between transmitters. If it cannot, the app records a total per rep and nothing finer. Everything below describes what is built and tested against generated frames. Do not expect it to work with a chip in your hand today.

What it does

A Freelap FxChip records the moment you cross each transmitter. This app takes those crossings over Bluetooth and puts them in your Garmin activity, so your splits sit in your normal training log rather than in a separate phone app.

The times in your file are the chip's own, carried across without being re-measured or re-derived. Freelap give the chip's accuracy as a hundredth of a second, which is what your phone shows you too — this app does not make it finer, it just puts it somewhere else. You describe your course once and the app works out the distances, velocity and pace for each split, and cuts one Garmin lap per Freelap rep.

What you need

A Freelap FxChip BLE
The watch cannot sense Freelap's magnetic transmitters itself. The chip does that; this app reads what the chip recorded.
Freelap transmitters
At least a start and a finish. More gives you more splits per rep.
A Garmin watch running Connect IQ 3.1 or later
With Bluetooth Low Energy available to apps. See the device list — not all of them have been tested.

What it looks like

Where your splits end up

In the activity's FIT file, as developer fields: one Garmin lap per rep with its totals, per-split fields on every record, and the session summary. They sync to Garmin Connect like any other activity, and two scripts pull them back out as CSV if you want the exact microseconds, which Connect's own display rounds.

Getting the splits out covers both routes and what every column means — including which single number in the file is an estimate rather than a measurement.

Privacy

Nothing leaves your watch. The app has no network permission and makes no network requests. It talks to your Freelap chip over Bluetooth Low Energy and writes to your own activity file, which syncs to Garmin Connect exactly as any other activity does. The app stores your course settings and the last session's splits on the watch, and the name of the chip you last connected to so it can prefer it next time. No analytics, no accounts, no third-party services.

Building it

You need the Connect IQ SDK and a developer key; there is nothing to install from a store yet. The build and test instructions are in the repository, and the design document explains how the whole thing fits together — the timing model, the FIT layout and the decisions behind them.

If you have an FxChip, a transmitter and a Garmin watch, the most useful thing anyone could do right now is point them at each other and record what happens — above all, whether the watch can see the splits between transmitters at all. Everything downstream of that is built and waiting.

Where this is up to

Freelap were asked for the protocol and said yes. What they sent replaced the guesswork this project began with, and it changed the shape of it: the chip does not hold a conversation with anything. It announces, briefly, and whatever happens to be listening hears it. So there is no pairing step, no connection to drop, and no reason two devices cannot follow the same chip at once.

The specification is theirs and is not published here. It is implemented, not republished.

Built and tested

  • A decoder for the chip's broadcasts, written twice — once in Python as the reference, once in Monkey C for the watch — with a test that fails the moment the two stop agreeing.
  • The part that decides which of the frames arriving are new efforts, which are the same effort announced again, and which belong to the athlete training beside you. It also notices when an effort was never heard at all, and says so.
  • Telling the watch which chip is yours, by the code printed on it, entered once in Garmin Connect.
  • A generator that produces chip frames on a laptop, so most of the work needs no hardware.

What happens next

Nearly everything remaining needs a chip, a transmitter and a wrist. The first item decides what this app can be, and the rest wait behind it.

  1. Find out whether a Garmin watch can hear the whole broadcast. The chip sends the finishing time in one part and the intermediate times in another, and no Garmin documentation says whether a watch receives the second part. If it does not, this records one time per effort rather than a time at every transmitter — useful, but not what is described above.
  2. Check the specification against a real chip, including two details it leaves genuinely ambiguous.
  3. Remove the old connection code, once a chip has confirmed it refuses connections.
  4. Twenty efforts on a track, against the phone app. Until that agrees, nothing here should be believed.
  5. Ride along inside a Garmin workout. A workout step ends when GPS says the distance is done; a Freelap transmitter ends it when you cross the line. Recording both against the same step would put the difference in your file.
